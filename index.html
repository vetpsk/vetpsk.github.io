From zoonotic spillover in a warming world to the economics of herd health, I study disease where animals and people meet.

I'm a veterinary epidemiologist working at the intersection of animals, people, and their shared environment. I build a range of One Health models, each designed to answer a different question: how infections move through multiple hosts and ecosystems, where outbreaks and spillovers are likely to occur, what burden a disease carries, and how climate change and policy reshape animal and disease systems. Depending on the question, I use statistical models, supervised and unsupervised machine learning, or mechanistic mathematical models.

In the news

Our 2026 study on climate and arenavirus spillover was picked up by outlets around the world, from Science News to The New York Times. A follow-up preprint takes the same approach to hantaviruses worldwide.

Climate-driven changes in zoonotic risk of arenaviral hemorrhagic fevers in South America

Kulkarni PS, Flores-Pérez NY, Jian AH, Bird BH, Johnson CK, Uhart M, Pandit PS. npj Viruses 4, 23 (2026).

Three rodent-borne arenaviruses, Guanarito virus (GTOV), Machupo virus (MACV) and Junín virus (JUNV), cause hemorrhagic fevers in South America that can kill up to about a third of patients. They get far less attention than their African relative, Lassa virus. We combined machine-learning habitat models for six rodent reservoirs with climate projections for 2041–2060 and human population data to estimate where people's risk of infection is likely to rise.

The headline: risk doesn't just grow, it moves, into places where doctors and public health systems have never had to deal with these diseases.

  • Guanarito virus (GTOV)Venezuelan HFProjected to spread beyond central Venezuela into Colombia, northern Brazil, and the border regions of Suriname.
  • Machupo virus (MACV)Bolivian HFProjected to climb from the Bolivian lowlands into the Andean foothills and mountains.
  • Junín virus (JUNV)Argentine HFProjected to shift within Argentina, easing in some grasslands while rising in new areas.

      Research vision

      Diseases that cross between animals and people are among the hardest to predict, track, or stop. They move through multiple hosts, travel on vectors, and shift with climate and land use in ways no single dataset captures. As a veterinarian and quantitative modeler, I build frameworks that make these systems predictable enough to act on.

      Find the risk

      Statistical and machine learning tools that turn disease data into early warnings, identify vulnerable communities, and guide where limited resources go.

      Model the system

      Models of complex multi-host and vector-borne disease that link emergence and spread to changes in climate, environment, and land use.

      Test what works

      Rigorous study designs that show whether health policies deliver what they promise.

      Supporting all three: sound data collection, stronger causal inference, carefully curated data, and open-source code that others can adapt.

      This work is only useful in partnership with public health officials, local governments, and community leaders; with agencies such as USDA-APHIS; and with virologists, entomologists, clinicians, economists, immunologists, and epidemiologists worldwide.

      Methods I use

      Species distribution modeling, spatio-temporal analysis, supervised and unsupervised machine learning, agent-based and herd simulation, survival analysis, Bayesian optimization, Markov decision processes and dynamic programming, and Monte Carlo simulation.

      I program mainly in R and Python, with QGIS and JavaScript for Google Earth Engine for spatial work, and Bash scripting for running analyses at scale. I build interactive web apps with Shiny and Streamlit, and also work with SAS and online survey platforms.
